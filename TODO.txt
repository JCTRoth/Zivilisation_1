even if your tiles are producing a food surplus.With a Granary:
The city is 100% immune to the random Famine disaster.

Here is the technical breakdown of the Civ 1 transport backend systems, adapted strictly for your game's specific rules (removing stack death and adding the unit selection modal):
## 1. The Active Container & Array System
In the Civ 1 engine, units do not possess an "embarked" flag status like in modern games. Instead, the game relies on a strict coordinate alignment :
* **Tile Sharing Logic:** A land unit is considered "on board" a transport ship if its grid coordinates (X, Y) match the transport's grid coordinates exactly over a water tile.
* **Capacity Cap Enforcement:** The engine actively checks the transport's data array capacity whenever a land unit moves onto its tile. If a ship already has its maximum number of land units sharing its X,Y coordinate, the engine treats that tile as an impassable barrier, completely blocking movement.
* **The sleeping Automatic Boarding:** When a transport ship exits a city tile, the code executes a query check for any land units flagged as sleeping (S key) or Sleep inside that city. If found, the engine changes their coordinates to match the moving ship, dragging them along as cargo automatically.

### 🆕 Unit Selection Modal (Cargo Management UI)
Because multiple units can share the exact same transport tile, a **Unit Selection Modal** is required for player interaction. 
* **Trigger:** When a player selects a transport ship and issues an "Unload" or "Activate" command.
* **Functionality:** A modal popup appears displaying the icons and stats of the land units currently assigned to that ship's coordinates. The player selects the specific unit they wish to wake up or unload, preventing accidental activation of the wrong unit in a crowded tile.

## 2. Deep Ocean Sinking Formula (Trireme Math)
The early-game Trireme uses a specific probability check if it finishes its turn on a deep ocean tile (dark blue) rather than a coastal tile (light blue):
* **The Cargo Wipe:** If the RNG determines the ship sinks, the engine executes a deletion loop for that tile's coordinates. It deletes the ship unit first, then systematically deletes every single land unit ID sharing those exact coordinates. **If the ship sinks, all units inside the ship are destroyed instantly.**
* **The Lighthouse Counter:** Building The Lighthouse World Wonder modifies this logic. The wonder acts as a flag modifier that shifts the Trireme’s sinking probability function to 0%, making early-game deep-ocean transport perfectly safe.

## 3. Combat Resolution (Individual Defense)
*Note: The classic Civ 1 "Death Stack" mechanic does not exist in this game.*
* **Individual Combat:** When an enemy naval unit attacks a tile containing your transport ship and any escort vessels, combat is resolved strictly on a 1-on-1 basis. The attacker engages the specific unit occupying the defensive slot; escort ships do not automatically absorb hits for the transport.
* **Destruction Consequence:** If the transport ship is directly attacked and loses the combat resolution calculation, it is destroyed. Because of the Cargo Wipe logic, all land units stored in its coordinate array are instantly destroyed alongside it. 

## 4. The Infinite Movement Exploit: "Ship Chaining"
Because of how the Civ 1 engine resets and checks unit movement points (MP), players can take advantage of a logic loophole:
* **The Movement Variable Reset:** When a transport ship moves, it consumes its own MP. However, the land units sitting inside the transport do not consume their land-based MP.
* **The Relay Hand-off:** If you park two transport ships on the exact same tile, you can manually "wake up" (W key) the land units inside Transport A. The engine sees them as standing on a valid transport tile, so they still have full movement points. You can then command them to "Sleep" again, and the engine will register them as occupying Transport B.
* **The Infinite Loop:** By lining up ships across the entire ocean map—each spaced exactly at the maximum movement range of the previous ship—you can hand off units from ship to ship. The engine allows you to move an army across a massive ocean map in a single turn, bypassing the turn timer mechanics entirely.

There are exactly 23 unique Wonders. Each can be built only once in the entire game, by only one civilization. Once a civilization completes a Wonder, no other civilization can ever build that same Wonder.
How construction works

Wonders are produced in a city exactly like regular city improvements or units (using production/shields).
Each Wonder requires a specific technology to unlock.
They cost a fixed number of shields (200–600).
They have no maintenance cost.
Multiple cities (even belonging to different civilizations) can work on the same Wonder at the same time, but only the first one to finish it gets it. Progress in other cities is then wasted (or converted in some versions, but in original Civ 1 it is generally lost).
You cannot sell a Wonder.
Capturing a city that contains a Wonder transfers ownership (and its effects) to the conqueror.

Obsolescence
Many Wonders become obsolete when any civilization discovers a certain later technology. When that happens, the Wonder’s special effect stops working for its owner (though the Wonder still remains for scoring and historical purposes).
Some late-game Wonders never become obsolete.
Effects and scope
Effects vary widely:

Some affect only the city that built them.
Some affect all your cities.
Some affect only cities on the same continent.
A few have global effects (e.g., enabling spaceships or nuclear weapons for everyone who has the required tech).

Wonders also give a significant score bonus at the end of the game.

- Wonders are produced in a city exactly like regular city improvements or units (using production/shields).
- Each Wonder requires a specific technology to unlock.
- They cost a fixed number of shields (200–600).
- They have **no maintenance cost**.
- Multiple cities (even belonging to different civilizations) may work on the same Wonder simultaneously.
- Only the first city to complete it obtains the Wonder. All other cities that were building it lose their progress (progress is wasted, matching original Civ 1 behavior).
- Wonders **cannot** be sold.
- Capturing a city that contains a Wonder transfers ownership (and all effects) to the conquering civilization.

**Obsolescence**
- Many Wonders become obsolete when *any* civilization discovers a specific later technology.
- When a Wonder becomes obsolete, its special effect stops working for its owner.
- The Wonder itself remains for scoring and historical purposes.
- Some late-game Wonders never become obsolete.

**Effects and scope**
- Effects can be:
  - City-only
  - Civilization-wide
  - Continent-wide
  - Global (e.g. enabling spaceships or nuclear weapons for everyone who has the required technology)
- Wonders grant a significant score bonus at the end of the game.

**Important note**  
The three ages (Antiquity / Middle Ages / Industrial-Modern) are **only for historical flavor and documentation**. They have **no influence** on gameplay mechanics.  
Wonders are listed in chronological order of their real-world inspiration purely for documentation and flavor text.

# Trading Technology in Civilization 1 (1991)

Quick prerequisites before any trading can happen:

- **You must have met the civilization** (contact must be established).
- **Both sides need something to swap** — Civ 1 trades are strictly one tech for one tech. No multi-tech deals, no cash sweeteners.

---

**Step 1 — Make contact (map screen)**
Move any of your units onto a square occupied by another civ's unit, or into one of their cities. A popup announces the meeting (e.g., *"You have made contact with the Russians!"*) and you're usually taken straight to the leader screen. This works both ways — an AI unit bumping into yours triggers the same dialogue.

**Step 2 — The Foreign Minister screen (F3)**
To talk to a civ again later, press F3 (in the DOS original; ports vary). This screen lists every civilization you've met, with leader names and government types. Highlight the civ you want and choose the option to talk/negotiate.

**Step 3 — The leader negotiation screen**
A portrait of the AI leader appears with a menu of proposals, roughly:
- Exchange of Knowledge
- Propose Peace (if at war)
- Propose Alliance (if at peace)
- Demand Tribute
- Declare War
- End Talks

Select **Exchange of Knowledge**. If you're at war with them, don't be surprised if they refuse to do business until peace is made.

**Step 4 — The Exchange of Knowledge screen**
Here the game shows technologies in two groups: **techs they have that you lack**, and **techs you have that they lack**. Sometimes the AI opens by proposing a specific swap itself; otherwise you pick one tech from each side — one to receive, one to give in return.

**Step 5 — Their verdict**
The AI weighs the two techs against each other (roughly by research cost and era). If the deal looks fair, it accepts and both techs change hands instantly. If your offer is too cheap in its eyes, it refuses — come back with something richer and ask again.

**Step 6 — Confirm the result**
Back on the map you'll get a confirmation message. The new tech appears as known on your Science Advisor screen and in the Civilopedia. Your current research continues as normal, but new options may have unlocked. You can usually keep talking and make additional swaps as long as both sides still have techs the other wants.

---

**Worth knowing:**

- **AI civs trade techs among themselves** during their turns, so knowledge spreads around the world even if you never talk to anyone.
- **The Great Library** wonder automatically grants you any advance known by at least two other civs — free "trades" with no diplomacy required.
- **The dishonest alternative:** walk a Diplomat into one of their cities and steal a technology (random pick) — instant knowledge, guaranteed diplomatic consequences.
- Friendlier leaders tend to be more willing to deal, so keeping relations civil pays off.
