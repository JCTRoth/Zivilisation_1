== Terminology — the term I propose

=== The term: *Doctrine*

Each civ gets a named personality package: **the Doctrine**. Its diplomatic
rule-set — the thresholds and predicates that decide war, peace, alliance,
tribute, treaties, gifts and betrayal — is **the Charter**.

[source,text]
....
DOCTRINE  "The Flower-War Doctrine"   <- civ identity: archetype + traits + charter + agenda + dossier
├── ARCHETYPE   military_expansion    <- today's StrategyProfile (behavioural family)
├── TRAITS      aggression 8 · diplomacy 3 · military 8 · expansion 8 · science 4 · economy 5
│                                            <- today's Civilization.personality (1-10)
├── CHARTER     doW 1.4x · tribute 1.8x · no peace offers · breaks non-aggression pacts
│                                            <- NEW: replaces the hard-coded thresholds
├── SIGNATURE   attacks launched from Forest/Jungle/Hills get +1 attack
├── AGENDA      "Eight cities, and no neighbour left untributed."
└── DOSSIER     title, portrait scene, greeting/demand/refusal lines
                                            <- today's LeaderPortraits.ts, extended
....

Why *doctrine* and not something else:

[cols="2,3"]
|===
| Term | Verdict

| *Doctrine*
| The industry term for a package of playstyle rules that decides how a faction
acts — Paradox (EU4/HOI4) use it for exactly this, and `doc/space-bunny-free_ai_gov.adoc`
already names the AI's goal set the *doctrine*. Adopting it unifies the two
documents instead of inventing a second word.

| *Charter*
| The right sub-term for the diplomatic half specifically: a standing set of
binding rules of engagement. A leader's *Charter* is what you learn by playing
against them.

| Unique Ability / Unique Trait (Civ V)
| Too narrow. It names a single bonus; we need a rule-set.

| Legacy (Civ V)
| Wrong meaning — in Civ V a *Legacy* is what a dead civ leaves to its
successor. We want the leader's own character.

| National Spirit (HOI4)
| Wrong polarity — a spirit is a temporary state modifier. A Doctrine is
permanent and identity-bearing.

| Archetype
| Right word, wrong level: an archetype is the *template* (there are 6 of
them). The instance is a Doctrine.

| Agenda
| Already used in the layer cake for the goal, which is what it means in Civ V.
Keep it there.

| Profile / Personality
| *Personality* is already taken in the codebase for the six 1–10 numbers
(`AITypes.Personality`), so those become *Traits* and the name is freed.
*Profile* says nothing.

| Behaviour / AI config
| Not player-facing language.
|===

== Why diplomacy is the layer to hang civ identity on

=== Today, choosing a civ changes nothing

The civ picker offers 14 civilizations (`GameSetupModal.tsx:119-122`), each with
its own colour, icon, leader and 40–70 city names. Mechanically the choice is
**cosmetic**. The evidence: every single read of `personality` and
`productionProfile` in the codebase reads *the acting civ's own* value —

`GovernmentManager.ts:100`, `DiplomacyManager.ts:181` (`fromCiv.personality`),
`DiplomacyManager.ts:683`, `AI/AIAggression.ts:122`, `AI/AIBuildingStrategy.ts:96-101`,
`AI/AIResearch.ts:187-192`, `AI/AIStrategySelector.ts:64-69`,
`AI/AIManager.ts:2401,3744,3799,4082`, `AutoProduction.ts:903,1019`

— and *all of those are AI code paths for the civ that is running the AI*. No
code anywhere reads a **foreign** civ's doctrine
(`grep -rn "otherCiv\.\(personality\|productionProfile\)" src/` → 0 hits).
The human's civ is never the actor, so its profile is never consulted.

Worse, the profile is chosen by **slot index**, not by civ
(`getCivProductionProfile(i)`, `AITypes.ts:135-137`, called from
`GameEngine.ts:1050-1051`). So the same civ plays differently depending on where
it lands in the shuffled start order, and the human's civ is always slot 0 →
always `early_expansion`.

=== Three reasons diplomacy is the right layer

. **It is the only layer that is identical on all 12 map types.** Production
  depends on terrain, tile counts and coastlines; research depends on what is
  reachable. The diplomatic ruleset reads only turn number, strength ratio,
  treaties and reputation — so a Doctrine behaves the same on `EARTH`
  (180×90), on `CLOSEUP_1V1` (20×20), on `NAVAL_CLOSEUP` (mostly water) and on
  `AI_VS_AI_NAVAL` (8 islands). A civ must be playable in all 168 civ × map
  combinations; the charter is the only place that can guarantee it.
. **It is the layer the player actually reads.** The diplomacy screen is the
  only place a leader's name, title, portrait scene, attitude, reputation,
  strength ratio and event history appear together (`GameModals.tsx:740-1012`).
  A Doctrine that only touched `determineProductionItem` would be invisible.
. **It is unfinished, so the differentiation is free.** Attitude has no memory
  (`getAttitude` recomputes from scratch, `:175-231`), willingness reads no
  personality at all (`:869-932`), refusing an offer costs nothing, peace is
  unconditional, there is no war weariness, gold gifting does not exist, tech
  exchange is unreachable from any UI (`:518-541`), and there is no third-party
  reaction to a war declaration. All of that is room to build in.

=== What changes for the player (the global gameplay effect)

* **Your civ's Charter is public.** It appears in the setup screen and in the
  diplomacy screen, and the AI follows it *toward you*. A Huns player is
  treated by the exact same rules as a Huns AI.
* **Attitude stops being one-sided.** Today `getAttitude(from, toward)` reads
  only the *actor's* traits (`:181-188`), so being a Huns makes people angry at
  you and being a Gandhi makes them like you — for no reason. The Charter adds
  a *target* term: fear of a strong doctrine, contempt for a weak one, warmth
  for a shared school.
* **The leaders start behaving differently from each other in the first 10
  turns**, instead of all of them offering an alliance or nothing.
* **The player's own aggression has a price.** `notoriety` (§ world-opinion
  ledger) means a player who plays the Grande Armée Doctrine loses allies
  whether or not they wanted them.
* **No leader is a pushover and no leader is unkillable.** Every Charter is
  exploitable: Gandhi will *not* retaliate, Caesar will *not* break a pact, and
  Shaka has literally one move.

== The data model

[source,typescript]
----
/** src/data/LeaderDoctrines.ts — one entry per civ, keyed by civ name. */
export type DiplomacySchool =
  | 'hegemon'        // takes what it wants; tribute or conquest
  | 'merchant'       // trade, open borders, no war without a reason
  | 'neutral'        // arbiter; will not initiate, will not be pushed
  | 'nonviolent'     // never initiates; coalition + tech trade
  | 'autocrat';      // cold, transactional, remembers refusals

export interface LeaderCharter {
  school: DiplomacySchool;

  /** Declare war when own/their strength ratio >= this. Infinity = never. */
  doW: {
    conquestRatio: number;
    /** The `aggression >= 7 && hostile` DoW, per-leader. Infinity = never. */
    hostileRatio: number;
    /** Ignore this many turns of peace after making peace (default 0). */
    noWarTurnsAfterPeace: number;
    /** Max simultaneous wars this leader will open (default 2). */
    maxOpenWars: number;
    /** Only attack a civ that is already at war (opportunist). */
    onlyOpportunists: boolean;
  };

  /** What it demands to end a war. */
  peace: {
    /** Propose a ceasefire when outmatched by this ratio (default 1.3). */
    atWeakeningRatio: number;
    /** ...but only after this many turns at war (default 5). */
    afterTurns: number;
    /** 'white' = no conditions, 'tribute' = gold demand, 'city' = cede a city. */
    terms: 'white' | 'tribute' | 'city';
    /** Offer peace at all (the Napoleon "I need one city" rule). */
    minimumWarScore: number;
  };

  alliance: {
    offers: boolean;          // proposes alliances
    acceptsWillingnessBonus: number;   // added to calculateWillingness
    /** Chance per 5-turn check to betray a pact of N turns standing. */
    betraysAfterTurns: number | null;
    betrayalChance: number;   // 0-100
    /** 'defensive' = today's alliance cascade; 'joint' = calls for help. */
    helpClause: 'none' | 'defensive' | 'joint';
  };

  tribute: { demands: boolean; atRatio: number; afterTurns: number;
             /** Amount multiplier, and +25% per refusal (Stalin). */ scale: number; escalates: boolean; };

  /** Gold and technology flows. Most are TODO.txt:4-8, unwired today. */
  trade: { offersTreatyAfterTurns: number; goldPerTurn: number;
           giftGoldWhenPoor: number; sellsTechForGold: number; stealsTech: boolean; };

  /** New offer types this leader may send. */
  offers: DiplomatAction[];   // e.g. ['demand_non_aggression'] for Napoleon

  /** Lines. Replace the fixed strings at :702/:709/:742/:753 and getRejectReason :955. */
  tone: { greeting: string; demand: string; peace: string; refuse: string; betray: string; };
}

export interface LeaderDoctrine {
  civ: string;               // 'Zulus' — matches Civilization.name
  leader: string;
  name: string;              // 'The Buffalo Doctrine'
  tagline: string;           // one line for the setup card
  archetype: StrategyProfile;
  traits: Personality;
  signature: { name: string; text: string };
  agenda: string;
  charter: LeaderCharter;
  dossier: { title: string; scene: LeaderPortraitConfig['scene']; brief: string };
}
----

`CIVILIZATIONS` entries stay untouched; the doctrine table is a parallel
`Record<name, LeaderDoctrine>` so the join key stays `name` (as it already is in
`MiniMapRenderer.ts:105-112`, `SidePanel.tsx:133`,
`GameSetupModal.tsx:79,194`).

== The Charter's hooks into today's code

Every charter field replaces something that is currently hard-coded. This table
is the contract between the design and the implementation.

[cols="2,2,3"]
|===
| Charter field | Replaces | Where

| `doW.conquestRatio`
| `conquestThreshold` = 1.6 / 2.0 / ∞ by archetype
| `DiplomacyManager.ts:720-723`

| `doW.hostileRatio`
| the hard-coded `1.5` in `classicDoW`
| `DiplomacyManager.ts:727`

| `doW.maxOpenWars`, `onlyOpportunists`
| *nothing* — a leader can open N wars at once, today
| —

| `peace.atWeakeningRatio`, `afterTurns`
| `theirStrength > ownStrength * 1.3 && turnsSince > 5`
| `DiplomacyManager.ts:702`

| `peace.terms`, `minimumWarScore`
| unconditional `makePeace`; the counter-offer's gold is computed and then dropped
| `DiplomacyManager.ts:709`, `:461-463`, `:843-849`

| `alliance.offers`
| `personality.diplomacy >= 6` (a trait used as a boolean)
| `DiplomacyManager.ts:748`

| `alliance.betraysAfterTurns`, `betrayalChance`

| `alliance.acceptsWillingnessBonus`, `trade.*`

| `DiplomacyManager.ts:869-932`

| *(new)* target-side attitude term

| `DiplomacyManager.ts:235-270`

=== Americans — Abraham Lincoln — *The Union Doctrine*

*Effect on gameplay.* Lincoln is the only leader in the game who will hand you an
alliance before you have earned it, and the only one you can grow past without
provoking. His conquest ratio is **3.0** — he needs to be three times your size
before he considers a war, and the tribute branch is disabled entirely. Five
turns after meeting you he proposes a trade agreement, then open borders, then
an alliance, in that order, regardless of who is stronger; and the alliance
offer itself is the most generous in the game — the strength gate is 0.5 (you
can be *half* his size and still qualify) and with `diplomacy 7` he clears the
`diplomacy >= 6` gate on every single check. He counters every alliance
proposal you send with a non-aggression pact instead — he wants the peace
without the obligation. He never offers you peace, ceasefire or tribute: he
simply never starts what he would have to end.

Because an alliance is *real* — attacking you drags every ally of yours into the
war (`GameEngine.ts:4013-4030`) — the Americans are the best shield in the game
and the best target for everyone else's first war. That is the whole design
intent: the United States is the civ other leaders want to be on the right side
of.

*If you play the Americans.* You start with an ally in every 7-civ game by turn
25 and a functioning trade web; what you give up is the cheapest way to end a
war by yourself — you cannot bully anyone into a tribute payment, ever.

*Counter-play.* A Lincoln civ has a small army and a 2-turn cycle between wars.
The classic play is to wait for his war to end, then attack him in the same
window: he cannot open a second front while at war, and his peace ask is
unconditional, so the cleanest peace is the one you offer first.

Charter notes:

* `doW.conquestRatio 3.0`, `tribute.demands false`
* `peace.terms 'white'`, `atWeakeningRatio 1.6`, `afterTurns 8` — he hangs on longer than anyone
* `alliance.offers true`, `acceptsWillingnessBonus +15` (the most trusted in the game)
* `alliance.betraysAfterTurns null` — Lincoln does not stab
* `trade.offersTreatyAfterTurns 5`, `goldPerTurn 2`
* Tone: *"We seek no quarrel with you, and we ask none of you."*

=== Aztecs — Montezuma — *The Flower-War Doctrine*

*Effect on gameplay.* You will be asked for money before you have been asked
for anything else. With `tribute.atRatio 1.8` and `doW.conquestRatio 1.4`, a
Montezuma civ is the most likely of all fourteen to open a war inside the first
30 turns, and the *first* thing it does at peace is demand tribute — the demand
fires as soon as it is 1.8× your strength and 10 turns past contact, which on a
slow map means you get the demand before your first city is finished. He never
proposes peace. When he is losing (1.6× for 8 turns) he offers a *ceasefire*
and then counters with a tribute demand of `max(20, strength × 5)` — the harshest
peace price in the game, because it scales with how badly he is losing, not with
how rich you are.

The Flower-War rule is the interesting one: a war he declares on a civ that has
broken a treaty with him is *free* (`notoriety +6` instead of +12), while a war
on an innocent neighbour is the most expensive in the game. This is the one
leader you can befriend as an aggressive player — a player who is already the
world's least popular can take the Aztecs as a partner without paying anything
extra for it.

*If you play the Aztecs.* Every AI knows you are dangerous from turn 1, so the
tribute demands are aimed at you too, and your wars are the ones other civs
refuse to join.

*Counter-play.* He is science 4 and signs anything you hand him — sign a
non-aggression pact, take one city, let him break it (−30 reputation,
`DiplomacyManager.ts:250-256`) and he turns on the next civ for you.

Charter notes:

* `doW.conquestRatio 1.4`, `hostileRatio 1.2`, `maxOpenWars 1`
* `peace.terms 'tribute'`, `atWeakeningRatio 1.6`, `afterTurns 8`
* `alliance.betraysAfterTurns 10`, `betrayalChance 30` — signs anything, breaks it routinely
* `signature` *flower war*: war on a treaty-breaker costs half notoriety
* Tone: *"The temple has weighed your offerings, and found them wanting."*

=== Babylonians — Hammurabi — *The Code Doctrine*

*Effect on gameplay.* Hammurabi is the only leader who cannot start a war at
all (`doW ∞`), and the only one whose willingness to accept your proposals does
not depend on how strong you are — he has no fear term, because he has no
conquest. Five turns after contact he proposes a trade agreement, then open
borders; he will sell you a technology for gold at `sellsTechForGold +15`
willingness, the highest rate in the game, which makes him the only civ you can
buy a technology from. He accepts mutual defence too. The result is that the
Babylonians are the best neighbour to have and the *worst* ally to hold, because
he also offers the same package to whoever is next to him — in a 7-civ game
Babylon eventually ends up allied to someone you are at war with.

*If you play the Babylonians.* Your peace is never in danger, your science is
the best in the game, and your cities are undefended. The one thing you must
watch: `expand 5` means an enemy can walk through you to reach someone else.

*Counter-play.* There is no defensive answer to a code — take his cities in the
first 60 turns while his army is four units. He will defend, he just will not
retaliate beyond his own borders.

Charter notes:

* `doW.conquestRatio Infinity`, `tribute.demands false`, `peace.terms 'white'`
* `alliance.offers true`, `acceptsWillingnessBonus +20`, `betrayalChance 0`
* `trade.sellsTechForGold 15`, `offersTreatyAfterTurns 5`
* `signature` *the Code*: no fear or contempt term in his attitude at all, ever
* Tone: *"A contract honoured is a wall built without a single stone."*

=== Chinese — Mao Tse Tung — *The Long March Doctrine*

*Effect on gameplay.* Mao signs nothing. Not alliances, not open borders, not
non-aggression, not trade agreements — all four are refused at `-30`
willingness, which is the single most reachable refusal in the game. His war
trigger is inverted relative to everyone else's: he declares at **1.2×** (the
cheapest war in the game) but he will not attack a civ more than **3×** stronger
than him, so what you see is a relentless series of small wars against whoever is
next to him, never a single decisive one. He demands tribute at 2×, and because
his demand amount is the fixed `max(25, ratio × 20)` it is the only demand in
the game you can budget for in advance. He never proposes peace, only a
ceasefire, and only when he is outmatched 1.5× for 10 turns. If you make peace
with him he re-opens the war after 20 turns at 15% per check — a Mao peace is a
20-turn ceasefire with a clause attached.

*If you play the Chinese.* Every neighbour knows you will eventually declare on
them, so the whole table reacts to you before you have done anything. In return
you get the one doctrine that never needs an ally: `doW 1.2` means you are always
fighting someone.

*Counter-play.* He is at war with someone in most turns of a 7-civ game, and he
never sues for peace. Do not give him a ceasefire; a war you refuse to end is
the only way to stop the 20-turn clock.

Charter notes:

* `doW.conquestRatio 1.2` with `strengthCeiling 3.0`, `hostileRatio ∞`
* `offers []` — he never sends an unsolicited offer except `demand_tribute`
* `peace.terms 'tribute'`, `atWeakeningRatio 1.5`, `afterTurns 10`
* `alliance.betraysAfterTurns 20`, `betrayalChance 15`; no alliance exists to betray
* Tone: *"The road is long. You are already on it."*

=== Egyptians — Ramesses II — *The Karnak Doctrine*

*Effect on gameplay.* The most boring leader in the game, on purpose. He never
declares a war, never demands tribute, and never opens a hostile act. What he
does is *mediate*: once he has seen you lose a war to anyone, there is a 20%
chance per 5-turn check that he offers you a ceasefire, and the offer goes
through the normal acceptance path — so accepting is a real, signed ceasefire
and not a trap. He is also the leader most likely to be an ally and least
likely to be attacked, which in a 7-civ game makes him the swing voter: a
Ramesses alliance is the alliance that decides who the hegemon does not touch.

*If you play the Egyptians.* You will spend the game being courted and almost
never be the reason anything happens. Your `wonder_rush` archetype means you
lose the actual race for territory, and the neutrality is not much compensation.

*Counter-play.* Neutral leaders are the *target*, not the threat — a Huns or
Zulu civ needs a tribute target and you are the one that will pay without a
war. If you cannot stand that, do not play Egypt; play a hegemon and take him.

Charter notes:

* `doW.conquestRatio Infinity`, `tribute.demands false`
* `signature` *the Karnak rule*: a 20% chance per check to offer a ceasefire to the loser of any war he can see
* `alliance.offers true` at `diplomacy 6`, `acceptsWillingnessBonus +10`
* `peace.terms 'white'`
* Tone: *"The walls have stood for three thousand years. So have we."*

=== English — Elizabeth I — *The Armada Doctrine*

*Effect on gameplay.* Elizabeth is the only leader whose diplomacy is a *ledger*.
Everything she does is priced: willingness to accept her proposals rises by +10
per 10 gold offered, capped at +25, so she is the only civ that can be bought;
she pays 4 gold/turn under a trade agreement instead of the treaty default of 2,
which makes English alliances the only alliance that pays for itself; and she
raises her own war threshold to 3.0 for the whole duration of a trade agreement,
so signing one with her genuinely removes her from the list of people who will
attack you.

Her one aggressive act is privateering: she demands tribute from a civ at 1.8×
*that is already at war with someone else* (`onlyOpportunists`), which is the
cheapest war in the game to start because you are already occupied. On
`AI_VS_AI_NAVAL` she is the strongest civ; on `CLOSEUP_1V1` the exact same
Charter runs and she is simply a rich merchant with a small army. That is the
map-agnostic guarantee doing its job: the water is an advantage, never a
requirement.

*If you play the English.* You are the civ that can solve the diplomacy screen
with gold instead of armies — every offer is negotiable, and the trade web is
the strongest in the game. What you do not get is a fast war.

*Counter-play.* Break a trade agreement and you lose 5 attitude *and* gain a
permanent −15 per broken treaty (`:192-193`) — she is the leader with the best
memory of ingratitude, and `willingness` is reduced by `broken × 15`, so she
stops answering your offers entirely.

Charter notes:

* `doW.conquestRatio 3.0`, reduced to 2.0 by an active trade agreement
* `tribute.atRatio 1.8`, `doW.onlyOpportunists true`
* `alliance.acceptsWillingnessBonus +gold/10 (max 25)`, `betrayalChance 0`
* `trade.goldPerTurn 4`, `offersTreatyAfterTurns 5`
* Tone: *"We do business, sir. Terms are terms."*

=== French — Napoleon Bonaparte — *The Grande Armée Doctrine*

*Effect on gameplay.* The most player-facing Doctrine in the game, because
Napoleon is the only leader who writes to you before he fights you. Within five
turns of contact he sends an **ultimatum** — a new offer type,
`demand_non_aggression`: sign a non-aggression pact and pay 50 gold, or he
declares war. That single rule turns a French civ from a background threat into
a scheduled event on the calendar, and it is the only demand in the game the
player can simply pay.

If you do not pay, he declares at 1.4×, demands tribute at 2×, and — uniquely —
he only offers peace once he has *taken something*: `minimumWarScore 1` means his
`propose_peace` branch requires a captured city. So every French war is a war
with a public, visible end point, and the moment you lose a city to him the
offer arrives. That is the most exploitable tell in the game, and it comes for
free from the fact that his peace is the only conditional one in the table.

*If you play the French.* You will be asked for money by every leader within ten
turns and you will have to decide whether paying sets a precedent. Your `peace`
terms are `city`, so you *can* end a losing war by giving one city — the only
leader who lets you.

*Counter-play.* Pay the 50. It buys 15 turns and a pact he will honour for 20
(`betrayalChance 8`, the lowest of the hegemons). Do not pay and you are at war
with military 9, aggression 9, on turn 6.

Charter notes:

* `offers ['demand_non_aggression']`, `signature` *the ultimatum* (new offer type)
* `doW.conquestRatio 1.4`, `hostileRatio 1.3`, `maxOpenWars 2`
* `peace.terms 'city'`, `minimumWarScore 1`, `atWeakeningRatio 1.3`, `afterTurns 12`
* `alliance.betraysAfterTurns 20`, `betrayalChance 8`
* Tone: *"I have come to offer you a choice. There is no third one."*

=== Germans — Frederick the Great — *The Iron Crown Doctrine*

*Effect on gameplay.* Frederick is the neighbour who answers every war with a
counter-offer instead of a peace. He will not propose peace or a ceasefire
until he is outmatched 1.3× for **10** turns — double today's threshold of 5
(`DiplomacyManager.ts:702`) — and when he finally does, he counters with a
tribute demand of `max(20, strength × 5)`, so his surrender is priced and
rising. He declares war at 1.5× but with `maxOpenWars 1`: one front at a time,
always. A Prussian war is therefore a long, single-front, attritional thing, and
that is exactly what makes him the most predictable aggressor in the game.

He is also the only leader who comes to *you*: he sends a diplomat to propose a
non-aggression pact you did not ask for, and he accepts a mutual defence pact
after a 10-turn delay (the longest handshake in the game).

*If you play the Germans.* Your wars are slow, total and hard to escape; your
build order is walls first, and the whole table learns that attacking you costs
them a city per defender.

*Counter-play.* Take one city, then offer peace. He is rich and slow, and his
own peace ask is tribute rather than territory, so a fast single-city war is
cheap against him — the mirror of what he does to you.

Charter notes:

* `doW.conquestRatio 1.5`, `maxOpenWars 1`, `hostileRatio ∞`
* `peace.atWeakeningRatio 1.3`, `afterTurns 10`, `terms 'tribute'`
* `alliance.offers true` (unsolicited non-aggression), `acceptsWillingnessBonus +5`
* `trade.offersTreatyAfterTurns 15` — the last to open relations
* Tone: *"I did not seek this war. I will end it on my terms, or not at all."*

=== Greeks — Alexander the Great — *The Anabasis Doctrine*

*Effect on gameplay.* Alexander is the most negotiable conqueror in the game,
and it is entirely a diplomacy-layer trick. His declaration of war is always
preceded by an offer — a 20-turn non-aggression pact priced at 100 gold, which
auto-accepts if you pay. So there is a window in which an Alexander civ is
bought off instead of fought, and the window is a fixed, learnable number. When
the war does come it is at 1.5×, and he offers *unconditional* peace the moment
he is 1.3× ahead — the only hegemon in the game whose peace is free.

His second rule is the most distinctive in the table: a 25% chance per 5-turn
check that he offers an **alliance to a civ he is currently at war with**. The
offer both signs the alliance and ends the war (a Pax offer). It is the only
mechanism in the game by which a war ends in an alliance, and it is why the
Greeks are terrifying in a long game: a war with Alexander is a recruitment
strategy.

*If you play the Greeks.* You fight constantly and lose nothing permanent. A
Græcan alliance eventually drags you into everyone else's wars, which is either
the strongest army in the game or a fast death.

*Counter-play.* Buy him off, and keep the 100 gold ready. He will ask again.

Charter notes:

* `signature` *the ultimatum offer*: war declarations are preceded by a priced pact, auto-accepted on payment
* `signature` *Pax*: 25% per check to offer alliance-and-peace to a civ at war
* `doW.conquestRatio 1.5`, `peace.terms 'white'`, `atWeakeningRatio 1.3`
* `alliance.betraysAfterTurns null`, `betrayalChance 0` — he keeps every pact
* Tone: *"Come with me, and you will see the world. Refuse, and I will take it anyway."*

=== Indians — Mahatma Gandhi — *The Sarvodaya Doctrine*

*Effect on gameplay.* The civ you cannot take and should not. Gandhi cannot
declare a war (`doW ∞`) and cannot demand tribute. Every offer he makes is
`+25` willingness, the highest rate in the game, so his proposals are the
cheapest to accept — and he is the only leader who *gives*: after 10 peaceful
turns he offers 25 gold if your treasury is under 20 ("to fund your schools"),
and he sells a technology for gold at `+20`. In practice the Indians are the
economically safest neighbour in the game and the only one that helps you.

The balancing rule is the important part, and it is what makes four hegemon
Doctrines legal in the same game: **attacking Gandhi costs you the room.** Every
civ that is not currently at war with you takes a −6 attitude hit the moment
you declare, permanently, and a civ whose `helpClause` is `joint` will open a
mutual defence against you within 5 turns. Attacking him is always possible, it
just costs you every alliance you have, and his defence is four units, so the
maths is completely in the player's hands.

*If you play the Indians.* You will never be attacked, you will be the most
trusted civ in the game, and you will be unable to take a single city without
paying a diplomatic price that the game does not itemise. `stealsTech: true`
means your diplomats are a real tool, not a ceremony — this is `TODO.txt:4-5`
wired to one leader.

*Counter-play.* There isn't one, by design. That is the point: the nonviolent
Doctrine is not weak, it is *expensive to violate*. A player who wants to
conquer must accept becoming the world's enemy, which is a real cost in a 7-civ
game and the reason the hegemons stay balanced.

Charter notes:

* `doW.conquestRatio Infinity`, `tribute.demands false`, `peace.terms 'white'`
* `alliance.acceptsWillingnessBonus +25`, `offers true`, `betrayalChance 0`
* `trade.giftGoldWhenPoor 25`, `sellsTechForGold 20`, `stealsTech true`
* `signature` *satyagraha*: attacking this civ costs every third party −6 attitude
* Tone: *"We have no army to impress you with. We have only the truth."*

=== Huns — Dschingis Khan — *The Steppe Storm Doctrine*

*Effect on gameplay.* The world enemy, and the only leader whose Doctrine has
no off switch. `doW.conquestRatio 1.2` is the cheapest war trigger in the game
and it is aimed at *everyone*, not at the weak: his casus belli is the steppe
having a neighbour. He demands tribute at 1.8× — a lower bar than anyone else's,
so you will be asked for money even when you are nearly his match — and he never
proposes peace at all, only a ceasefire, only when outmatched 1.6×. If you do
make peace he re-opens the war after 20 turns at **25%** per check, three times
the current rate (`:764-765`).

This is why the Doctrine needs a *cost*, and `notoriety` is it: each war adds
+12 notoriety (+25 if from an alliance, +30 if from a ceasefire), the whole
table reads it, and at ≥ 40 every non-hegemon civ opens a mutual defence against
him within 5 turns; at ≥ 60 the coalition declares. So a Huns civ is either a
world war from turn 40 or a civ that has burned out. Attacking him while he is
already at war costs half the notoriety, which is the only cheap way in.

*If you play the Huns.* You will be at war with everyone by turn 30 and the
world will unite against you by turn 60 unless you have taken someone with you
in the first 40. The doctrine is the highest-variance in the game and it is the
only one where the *player* has to solve the diplomacy layer or the game
resolves itself.

*Counter-play.* Alliance early — the English, the Romans and the Indians will
all take you, because all three offer `helpClause: 'joint'` — and then refuse
every ceasefire. A war you refuse to end is the only weapon that beats a civ
that will not sue for peace.

Charter notes:

* `doW.conquestRatio 1.2`, `hostileRatio 1.2`, `maxOpenWars 6`
* `peace.terms 'tribute'`, `atWeakeningRatio 1.6`, `afterTurns 10`, no peace offers
* `alliance.betraysAfterTurns 20`, `betrayalChance 25`
* `tribute.atRatio 1.8`, `offers ['demand_tribute']` only
* Tone: *"The steppe does not recognise your borders. It recognises your horses."*

=== Romans — Julius Caesar — *The Legion Doctrine*

*Effect on gameplay.* Caesar is the only leader whose treaties are enforceable.
Every proposal you send is `+20` willingness — the highest acceptance modifier in
the game — and `betrayalChance` is 0 unless he is simultaneously at war with a
third civ, so an alliance or a trade agreement with Rome is the closest thing
this game has to a binding contract. Within 5 turns of contact he has proposed a
trade agreement, open borders and an alliance, in that order. He also *pays*:
`trade.goldPerTurn 4` on top of the treaty default, so a Roman alliance is the
only alliance with a positive income line, which is how a civ with
`aggression 6` and `military 8` funds its own wars.

The price is a very high bar for war: `doW.conquestRatio 2.2` for an army of 8.
He is the second most dangerous defensive civ in the game and the most
expensive one to invade, because his peace is unconditional and he never asks
you for anything except tribute at 2× — which, on the highest gold income in the
game, is the smallest actual sum in the table.

*If you play the Romans.* You are the civ that can fund an ally, and the only
one the AI will believe a treaty with. You are also the civ that must win its
war quickly, because `doW 2.2` is a doctrine that punishes a long one.

*Counter-play.* There is no cheap war against Rome. The only attack is
diplomatic: make him the aggressor by gifting him gold and a friendly attitude,
then let `doW 2.2` do the rest of the work for you.

Charter notes:

* `alliance.acceptsWillingnessBonus +20`, `betrayalChance 0` (unless at war elsewhere)
* `doW.conquestRatio 2.2`, `hostileRatio ∞`
* `trade.goldPerTurn 4`, `offersTreatyAfterTurns 5`, `giftGoldWhenPoor 0`
* `signature` *foedus*: treaties pay 4/turn instead of 2, and are never broken
* Tone: *"I am prepared to treat with you — on terms that survive both of us."*

=== Russians — Joseph Stalin — *The Five-Year Plan Doctrine*

*Effect on gameplay.* The only leader with a *memory*. Refusing a Stalin tribute
demand costs 8 attitude, permanently — `reputationModifier` decay of 1/turn is
switched off for him, and that is the only reason the demand is a real decision
instead of a free roll (`DiplomacyManager.ts:628-635` recovers both signs toward
0 today, and nothing ever grants positive reputation, so the positive branch is
dead code). His demand *escalates by 25% per refusal*, so the second demand is
1.25× the first and the third is 1.56×. He demands tribute at 1.8× — the lowest
of the autocrats — and declares war at 1.5× but only while he is already at
peace, so the table sees him attack exactly once, completely, and then stop
starting wars for a long time.

He accepts nothing as a standalone offer: a non-aggression pact is only
acceptable if bundled with a trade agreement (`packageDeals: true`), and he
never proposes peace or alliance to anyone. He sues for peace only at 1.6× with
a tribute demand attached.

*If you play the Russians.* You are the ideal neighbour for a moderate player —
you can pay the first demand and be left alone, and you will never be asked for
an alliance you do not want. What you cannot do is stall: the escalation clock
starts on the first refusal.

*Counter-play.* Out-tribute him. His demands are deterministic and his economy
is the second best in the game, so a rich civ can absorb three escalations and
buy the longest peace in the table.

Charter notes:

* `signature` *the escalation*: `+25%` demand per refusal, refusal = −8 permanent attitude
* `tribute.atRatio 1.8`, `escalates true`
* `doW.conquestRatio 1.5`, `maxOpenWars 1`, `hostileRatio 1.5`
* `peace.atWeakeningRatio 1.6`, `terms 'tribute'`, `alliance.offers false`
* `signature` *the package*: non-aggression only acceptable with a trade agreement
* Tone: *"There is no public opinion here. There are only plans."*

=== Zulus — Shaka — *The Buffalo Doctrine*

*Effect on gameplay.* Shaka is the control group: he has exactly one move. His
`offers` array contains only `demand_tribute`, so the only thing that will ever
appear in your diplomacy screen from a Zulu civ is a money demand — no alliance
proposal, no peace, no ceasefire, no counter-proposal, no treaty, no greeting
beyond the fixed one. He declares at 1.4×, demands tribute at 1.7×, and never
betrays an alliance because he never forms one. The absence of a diplomatic
state machine is the design: against Shaka, the other thirteen leaders look like
politicians, and the player learns what the diplomacy layer is actually doing by
contrast.

The one tool that works against him is `bribeUnit` (`:574-623`) — 60% base
chance, +20 if you are hostile, −20 if friendly, cost `25 × (attack + defence)`,
and it **auto-declares war** on success (`:619-622`), so a successful bribe is a
one-sided declaration that arrives with a stolen defender already in your army.
That is the cheapest war in the game and it works on precisely the leader who
has nothing else to offer.

*If you play the Zulus.* You get the fastest, cheapest, largest army in the game
and a completely empty diplomacy tab. Nobody will ever ally with you, nobody
will ever demand peace, and every war you start is a war you have to end
yourself.

*Counter-play.* Bribe the defender, take the city, and use his predictability:
he will demand tribute from you on a fixed 5-turn cadence and never respond to
anything else you say.

Charter notes:

* `offers ['demand_tribute']` — all other branches disabled
* `doW.conquestRatio 1.4`, `hostileRatio 1.3`, `maxOpenWars 3`
* `peace` — never proposes, never counters
* `alliance.offers false`, `betrayalChance 0` (no alliance possible)
* `signature` *iLifa*: the tribute demand is the only diplomacy he has
* Tone: *"Gold, or war. Choose quickly."*

== The world-opinion ledger

Four hegemons, three non-aggressors and six merchant/autocrat/neutral doctrines
cannot coexist in a 7-civ game unless aggression has a *price* that is visible
and paid in the same currency as diplomacy. `declareWar` today has no
third-party consequence at all (`:235-270`) — the world is passive, which is
precisely why the hegemon Doctrines would dominate if they were merely more
aggressive.

[source,typescript]
----
/** One number per civ, public, shown in the diplomacy screen. */
notoriety: number;   // 0-100, starts at 0
----

[cols="3,1,3"]
|===
| Event | Δ | Note

| Declare war from peace | +12 | `SURPRISE_ATTACK_PENALTY` already costs the *victim* −20 pair reputation
| Declare war from an alliance | +25 | the worst thing you can do
| Declare war from a ceasefire | +30 | breaks the 5-turn cooldown rule
| Refuse a tribute demand | +4 | the first real cost for a free action in the game
| Cancel a treaty | +3 | today this costs the pair −5 and nothing else
| Tribute paid | −6 | makes extorting expensive
| Alliance honoured (defender joins your war) | −10 | rewards the *merchant* doctrines
| Peace made | −2 |
| Non-aggression pact honoured for 10 turns | −3 |
|===

Consequences, all keyed off the leader you are dealing with:

* `notoriety < 40` — every civ starts its attitude toward you `−0.25 × notoriety`.
* `notoriety ≥ 40` — any civ with `helpClause: 'joint'` offers a mutual defence
  pact against you within 5 turns of the next check, if it is at war.
* `notoriety ≥ 60` — the coalition declares on the leader's turn.
* Hegemon Doctrines pay this constantly (4 of 14 have `doW ≤ 1.4`); nonviolent
  and merchant Doctrines bank it. That is the exchange rate that makes
  `Indians` a valid pick in a game that also contains `Huns`, and it is why the
  player's own choice of Doctrine is a real decision rather than a colour.

== The map-agnostic guarantee

Because the player may pick any civ on any of the twelve map types
(`GameSetupModal.tsx:279-291`), three rules make that safe.

=== Rule 1 — the Charter reads no terrain

A charter field may read: turn number, military strength ratio, treaty state,
reputation, notoriety, traits. Nothing else. No terrain, no map id, no tile
count, no coastline, no city count. Everything else in the game may read
terrain; the Charter may not. This is why the English get their naval advantage
from *trade treaties* rather than from ships.

=== Rule 2 — no Signature may require a terrain or a unit class

A Signature that references mountains, ocean or a specific tile is a civ that is
dead on four of the twelve maps. Every Signature in the fourteen above is
positional (attacking, defending, at war, at peace, rich, poor) or diplomatic
(a pact, an offer, a price). This is a hard rule, and it is the reason
`AI_VS_AI_NAVAL` and `CLOSEUP_1V1` are both playable with the Germans and the
Zulus.

=== Rule 3 — the stress map types must stay legal

Four of the twelve map types attack the whole premise of a Doctrine, and each
one gets an explicit charter clause:

[cols="2,3"]
|===
| Map type | What it breaks | Charter clause

| `NO_SETTLERS`
| No expansion is possible, so `expansion 8` is a wasted stat and the hegemons have no land to take
| `doW.casualtyRule: 'opportunist'` — a leader with `expansion ≥ 8` switches its trigger from conquest to `hostileRatio` and opens war at 1.3× regardless of expansion potential

| `MANY_CITIES`
| Four cities each, all borders touching: the hegemon Doctrines become the *most* powerful, not the least
| No clause needed — this is the map where the Huns win, and it should

| `TECH_LEVEL_10`
| No tech tree to shape, so the merchant/technocrat Doctrines lose their engine
| `trade.sellsTechForGold` becomes the *only* tech route, so the Babylonians and Indians are stronger here, not weaker

| `ALL_UNITS` / `CLOSEUP_BEATUP`
| Armies from turn 1, so every strength ratio is 1.0 and no conquest trigger fires
| `doW` falls through to the school default: hegemon 1.5, autocrat 1.6, merchant ∞, nonviolent ∞. Nobody declares on turn 1 — which is the correct outcome for a showcase map
|===

=== Verification matrix

Fourteen civs × six representative map types, run through
`AI_VS_AI_SMALL`-style headless sessions with the existing export harness
(`src/utils/GameProgression.ts`, `scripts/run-naval-session.mjs`):

[cols="2,2,2"]
|===
| Assertion | Threshold | Fails if

| No doctrine wins more than 45% of 20 sessions
| per civ, per map
| A hegemony or a non-aggression doctrine is strictly dominant

| Win-rate spread across the 14 doctrines on one map
| < 25 points between best and worst
| A doctrine is dead on a map type

| Wars declared per session
| 0.8 – 4.0
| A doctrine opens no war at all, or the table is permanently at war

| Peace treaties signed per session
| ≥ 0.4
| Wars can only be won, never ended — the current `AI_VS_AI_ANALYSIS` stalemate

| Mean turns at war
| < 45
| A Doctrine cannot end its own wars (the Huns clause)
|===

== Five bugs in the current diplomacy layer that this design has to fix anyway

Found while mapping the system; all five change how the leaders feel, and all
five sit in the Charter's path.

. **Reputation is stored on the shared pair record** (`applyReputationPenalty`,
   `:941-946`), but `getAttitude` reads it as `score += rel.reputationModifier`
   (`:194`) in *both* directions. A civ's own −20 surprise-attack penalty
   therefore lowers its attitude toward its own victim — the aggressor dislikes
   its victim less the more unjust the war was. Reputation must become directed
   (`reputationToward[civId]`) before any Charter is trustworthy.
. **"Contempt" is a friendly shift** (`:211`):
   `if (ownStrength > theirStrength * 2) score += 5;` — being three times the
   size of your neighbour makes them *like you more*. The comment says "more
   aggressive but not hostile"; the sign is backwards. This is why a player who
   out-grows everyone currently gets a world full of friends.
. **The ceasefire cooldown gates the penalty, not the war** (`:250-256`). A civ
   may legally declare war the turn after signing a ceasefire and pay nothing,
   because the check lives inside the penalty branch. That makes a ceasefire free
   to break, which makes the whole treaty layer decorative.
. **Refusing an offer costs nothing.** There is no insult tracking anywhere
   (`getRejectReason` is the only consequence, `:955-962`). Every diplomatic
   interaction is therefore free-riding, and the Stalin escalation rule in this
   document is the first feature to need a refusal cost.
. **There is no war weariness.** `peaceTurns` is a score field only
   (`VictoryManager.ts:155`); nothing in the engine wants peace. A war can only
   end by strength ratio or by turn 15, which is exactly the stalemate recorded
   in `doc/AI_VS_AI_ANALYSIS.md`. `peace.terms` and `minimumWarScore` need a
   `warScore` to compare against, and `warScore` is where "Stalin's war" vs.
   "Caesar's war" becomes a number the player can see in the diplomacy screen.

== UI surface

* **Setup screen** (`GameSetupModal.tsx:171-205`): each civ card shows the
  doctrine name, the tagline, the six traits as a 6-bar micro-chart, and the
  school icon (sword / scroll / balance / dove / star). This is the first time
  the player sees anything mechanical about a civ, so it is also the fix for
  the cosmetic-picker problem.
* **Diplomacy screen header** (`GameModals.tsx:782-812`): under the existing
  leader name, title and attitude badge, add the doctrine name and a
  "World Standing" bar for `notoriety`. The existing reputation chip
  (`:803-808`) becomes the *directed* reputation, so it can be negative for one
  civ and positive for another.
* **Offer banners** (`:722-734`, `:867-883`): the two flat accept/reject
  buttons become the offer's terms — "Napoleon Bonaparte demands 50 gold and a
  non-aggression pact. Refusing means war on your next turn." — generated from
  the Charter's `tone` and the offer's own terms, so no message text is
  hard-coded in the engine any more.
* **Negotiations block** (`:886-931`): buttons the target's Charter forbids are
  shown disabled with a reason, e.g. *Mutual Defence — Shaka signs nothing.* That
  single change is what makes fourteen different leaders legible.
* **Diplomat context menu** (`UnitActionsModal.tsx:215-258`): the five fixed
  actions become the leader's `offers` list, plus tech exchange and gold gift
  (`TODO.txt:4,6`), both of which exist in the engine and are unreachable today.
* **History panel** (`:970-1012`): three event types gain a leader's colour, so
  the log reads as a political history rather than a log of status changes.

== Implementation order

Each step is independently shippable and independently testable.

. **Move the doctrine data in, without changing behaviour.** New
  `src/data/LeaderDoctrines.ts` with all fourteen entries, keyed by civ name.
  `GameEngine.createCivilizations` (`:1026-1053`) reads the doctrine instead of
  `getCivProductionProfile(i)`, so the profile follows the civ rather than the
  slot. This alone fixes the index bug and makes the choice real. Tests:
  `tests/ai/leaderDoctrines.test.ts` — every civ has an entry, every entry has a
  valid archetype and 1–10 traits, no duplicate doctrine names.
. **Charter part 1 — thresholds.** Replace the hard-coded numbers in
  `processAIDiplomacy` with charter reads, keeping the current values as the
  default when a field is absent. No behaviour change for a civ without a
  charter. Tests: existing `tests/ai/DiplomacyManager.test.ts` and
  `tests/diplomacyOffers.test.ts` must pass **unchanged** — that is the proof
  the refactor is inert.
. **Charter part 2 — the attitude model.** Directed reputation, fix the contempt
  sign, add the target-side term. Tests: a new table-driven test over the whole
  attitude matrix.
. **Charter part 3 — willingness and tone.** `calculateWillingness` gains a
  per-leader bonus; `getRejectReason` and the five offer messages come from
  `tone`. Tests: acceptance thresholds per leader.
. **`notoriety` + the ledger.** New per-civ field, the ten event deltas, the
  `≥40` / `≥60` consequences, save/load. Tests: a session where a hegemon
  provokes a coalition.
. **Offers the engine already has but nobody can reach:** tech exchange
  (`:518-541`), and the missing validation from `TODO.txt:4` ("only if the tech
   is unknown to the receiver", "only when two units meet"). Add `offer_gold` and
   `request_gold` (`TODO.txt:6,8`) with the refusal cost they require.
. **The two new offer types** — `demand_non_aggression` (Napoleon's ultimatum)
  and the Pax offer (Alexander), both expressed as existing
  `DiplomacyProposal`s with a `goldAmount`, so no new plumbing.
. **War score and conditional peace** — the last piece, and the one that unlocks
  `peace.terms` and `minimumWarScore`. War weariness follows immediately, since
  `warScore` is the number both need.
. **UI pass.** Setup cards, doctrine header, disabled-forbidden buttons, World
  Standing bar, leader-tinted history.

== Open questions

* **Do the trait sliders stay 1–10?** They are read by production and research
  code as absolute values (`getPersonalityModifier` maps 1–10 → 0.6–1.4). If the
  Charter becomes the real identity, the sliders could be replaced by the
  Charter alone, which would let a leader be gentle at home and brutal abroad.
  That is a bigger refactor and is not required for this document.
* **Should the player's own Charter be visible to the AI?** Today the AI cannot
  see the human's doctrine at all, and cannot tell a Huns player from a Romans
  player. Letting the AI *know* the human's school is what makes e.g. the
  Indians' `satyagraha` clause work. The alternative — the AI only ever sees
  behaviour — is more realistic and much less legible.
* **Can a leader be talked out of a Doctrine?** E.g. a Huns civ that has lost
  three wars in a row sues for peace with real terms. Nice, and it is the natural
  use of `warScore` once it exists.
* **The Huns are named `Huns` in `GameData.ts:103` and `Kazakhs` in `README.adoc:198`
  and `GAMEPLAY.md:23`.** Before the doctrine table is written, pick one — the
  leader is `Dschingis Khan` either way, and the city names are Kazakh.
* **`bribeUnit` destroys the gold it takes** (`:611-621` deducts from the
  thief and never credits anyone). If bribery is going to be the counter-play
  against Shaka, that asymmetry needs fixing first.
