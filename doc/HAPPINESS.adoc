= Happiness & Disorder — How the City Knows Whether It Is Happy

The complete reference for the happiness system: every source of happiness,
every source of unhappiness, the exact disorder test, what disorder costs, and
every consumer of the result.

Every number below was read out of the source, not out of an older document.

[IMPORTANT]
====
If you are changing a happiness value, this is the file to update.
Source of truth per section is named in the "Defined in" note.
====

Where this document and `GAMEPLAY.adoc` or `GAME_RULES.adoc` disagree, *this
document* is right for happiness and disorder.

== The one-sentence version

A city is in disorder when its population is larger than everything it can
muster to keep it calm: `unhappiness > happiness`, where unhappiness is simply
the population and happiness is a sum of seven small terms. A disordered city
pays no tax, produces no science, and does not grow — but it still eats.

== The two numbers

`unhappiness` — *defined in* `EconomicManager.cityHappiness` (`src/game/engine/EconomicManager.ts:896`)

[source,typescript]
----
const unhappiness = population + capturedUnrest;
----

Exactly two terms:

[cols="1,3"]
|===
| Term | Value

| `population`
| The city's size, one unhappy citizen each. This is the whole rule.

| `capturedUnrest`
| `+3` (`CAPTURED_CITY_UNHAPPY`) while `city.capturedTurns > 0`. A freshly
  captured city resents its new owner. It decays by one turn per player turn
  (`TurnManager.processTurn`) and reaches zero on its own, so a captured city
  heals without player action.
|===

[NOTE]
====
There is **no government tolerance** in this game. It was removed: the
`tolerance` field is gone from `GovernmentProperties`, and the crowding rule is
plainly `population`. Governments now contribute only a flat *happiness bonus*
(see the table below), which is a much smaller lever than tolerance was — a
government with tolerance 5 used to swallow five unhappy citizens.
====

`happiness` — *defined in* `EconomicManager.cityHappiness`

[source,typescript]
----
const happiness =
  out.luxury +
  specLuxury +
  martialLawBonus +
  this.buildingHappiness(city) +
  gov.happinessBonus +
  BASE_CONTENTMENT;
----

Six additive terms. There are no multipliers, no diminishing returns and no
ordering effects — the sum is the score.

=== The terms in detail

==== 1. `out.luxury` — commerce converted by the luxury rate

*Defined in* `cityOutputs` (`EconomicManager.ts:228`).

[source,typescript]
----
luxury: Math.max(0, Math.floor(afterCorruption * (rates.luxury / 100)))
----

* `afterCorruption` is the city's commerce *after* the government's commerce
  penalty and *after* distance-from-capital corruption — see
  <<The luxury chain and why it is the fragile term>>.
* Luxury is `floor`ed, so a small city's luxury is worth nothing until it has
  real commerce. A size-2 city cannot buy its way out of unhappiness with a tax
  slider.
* Luxury survives disorder: `applyCityOutputs` deliberately does *not* zero it
  (see <<What disorder costs>>), which is what lets a disordered city climb back
  out instead of being trapped forever.

==== 2. `specLuxury` — Entertainers

*Defined in* `specialistYields` (`EconomicManager.ts:683`) from
`SPECIALIST_YIELDS` (`src/data/GameConstants.ts:283`).

[cols="1,1,1,1"]
|===
| Specialist | Luxury | Gold | Science

| Entertainer | *2* | 0 | 0
| Taxman | 0 | 2 | 0
| Scientist | 0 | 0 | 2
|===

An Entertainer is the only specialist that buys happiness, and it buys it by
*removing a worker from the fields* — the population does not change, so the
city is no less unhappy, but it now also earns 2 happiness. Entertainers are
therefore self-limiting in the AI (see <<AI: keeping cities calm>>).

==== 3. `martialLawBonus` — the garrison (martial law)

*Defined in* `cityHappiness`.

[source,typescript]
----
const garrisonUnits = this.garrisonOnCityTile(civ, city);   // EconomicManager
const martialLawBonus = Math.min(garrisonUnits, martialLawMax);
----

* Any *own* living unit with attack above 0 counts if it stands **on the city
  tile**, or if it is **fortified on or beside the tile**.
* A *fortified* defender one square out therefore does count. It was the normal
  way to garrison — entrench next to the walls for the +50% defense bonus — and
  it contributed nothing, because the test was an exact coordinate match. The
  unit was visibly defending the city and the city got no benefit from it.
* An *unfortified* unit merely standing nearby does still *not* count: one
  passing through, or marching past, is not a garrison.
* The bonus is capped by government, and the cap is checked by government
  *name*:

[cols="1,1"]
|===
| Government | `martialLawMax`

| Despotism, Anarchy | *4*
| Monarchy, Communism | *3*
| Republic, Democracy | *1*
|===

* Republic and Democracy used to be *0* — no martial law at all. They keep the
  law off their civilian cities, but they are not exempt from *defending* them,
  and a flat zero made garrisoning worthless for exactly the governments most
  likely to be fighting a war. One is deliberately the smallest possible bonus:
  a republic cannot buy its way out of unhappiness by posting soldiers, but a
  single defender is worth one content citizen, which is often exactly the
  citizen a taxman needs.
* Martial law is the cheapest happiness in the game: no gold, no worker, just a
  soldier who is not doing anything else. This is what makes garrison duty worth
  reserving — see <<AI: garrison duty>>.
* The rule lives in one place, `EconomicManager.martialLaw(civ, city)`, which
  returns `{ bonus, max, current }`, and the counting itself is one place,
  `garrisonOnCityTile(civ, city)`. Four callers need the same answer — the
  happiness total, the disorder check inside `processTurn`, the AI's luxury
  arithmetic, and the AI's garrison deficit — and when they were written out
  separately they drifted.

[IMPORTANT]
====
**A garrison point is dual-purpose, and this is where the common Civ 1 advice
is wrong for this game.** The widely-copied rule says *"units in cities are not
available for defense"* and advises moving them out to fight. Here they *are* the
defence: when a city is attacked, `GameEngine.attackCity` collects the living
units standing on the city tile as the garrison, fights them one at a time, and
only runs the population-vs-attack roll once the garrison is empty. A soldier
standing in a city both pacifies it and defends it, so there is no trade-off to
balance — parking a defender is not a cost.
====


==== 4. `buildingHappiness` — buildings

*Defined in* `buildingHappiness` (`EconomicManager.ts:954`), a plain sum of
`BUILDING_PROPERTIES[id].effects.happiness` over the city's buildings.

[cols="1,1,2"]
|===
| Building | Happiness | Note

| Temple | *+1*
| Courthouse | *+1*
| Palace | *+1*
| Hospital | *+1*
| Colosseum | *+2*
| Mass Transit | *+2*
| Cathedral | *+3*
| *City Walls* | *−1* | the only negative effect in the table
|===

[IMPORTANT]
====
**City Walls make a city unhappy.** Walls carry `happiness: -1` and it is summed
like any other value, so a walled city needs one more point of happiness than an
identical unwalled one. Walls also double city defence (`City.getDefenseValue`)
and shield the population from ground attacks, so the trade is usually worth it —
but it is a real cost and it is applied unconditionally.
====

==== 5. `wonderHappiness` — content from a wonder

*Defined in* `wonderHappiness` (`EconomicManager.ts:945`).

A wonder carrying `globalHappiness` grants content to *every* city of the civ
that owns it, not just the city it stands in. This is the empire-wide answer to
a crowded empire, and the only wonder effect that touches happiness at all:

[cols="1,1"]
|===
| Wonder | `globalHappiness`

| *Pyramids* | *+1* in every city
| Hanging Gardens | *+1* in every city
|===

Two implementation notes, both of which were bugs first:

* `globalHappiness` was declared on Hanging Gardens and *read by nothing* — the
  effect existed only on paper. It is now wired, and it was removed from
  `INERT_EFFECTS` so the building audit prices it instead of calling the wonder
  worthless.
* **Wonders live in `WONDER_PROPERTIES`, not `BUILDING_PROPERTIES`.** Looking a
  wonder up in the wrong table returns `undefined` rather than an error, so an
  effect read that way silently arrives as zero. Both `buildingHappiness` and
  `wonderHappiness` therefore go through one accessor, `buildingEffect(id, key)`,
  which consults both tables and coerces the value to a number (the effects bag
  has an index signature, so undeclared keys arrive as `unknown`).

Each global wonder is counted **once** per civ, not once per city holding one.

==== 6. `gov.happinessBonus` — the government

*Defined in* `src/data/GovernmentData.ts`.

[cols="1,1,1,1,1"]
|===
| Government | Happiness bonus | Tax cap | Corruption | Commerce penalty

| Despotism | *0* | 100 | 30 % | 0
| Monarchy | *+1* | 100 | 25 % | 0
| Republic | *+2* | 100 | 15 % | 0
| Democracy | *+4* | *10* | 5 % | 0
| Communism | *+1* | 100 | 10 % | *−25 %*
| Anarchy | *0* | 0 | 30 % | 0
|===

* Anarchy forces all three rates to 0 (`forcesZeroRates`), so during a
  revolution a city earns nothing at all and cannot buy luxury. The revolution
  itself lasts `ANARCHY_TURNS = 3` turns (`GovernmentManager.ts:17`).
* Republic and Democracy get no martial law but pay the highest bonuses.
  Republic is the best place to *stop* — which is exactly why the AI is told not
  to stop there (see <<AI: government target>>).

==== 7. `BASE_CONTENTMENT` — the floor

*Defined in* `EconomicManager.ts:85` as `2`.

Every city gets 2 free happiness, always, with no building and no government.
This is what makes a size-2 city content on its own.

== The disorder test

*Defined in* `cityHappiness`:

[source,typescript]
----
disorder: unhappiness > happiness
----

[IMPORTANT]
====
The comparison is **strictly greater**. A city with `unhappiness == happiness`
is *content* — it sits exactly on the knife edge, and the next citizen to move
in tips it over. This off-by-one is deliberate in the original game and is
pinned by tests (`tests/economicManager.test.ts`).
====

The AI treats the same knife edge as trouble. Two places consider a city
"unhappy" when `disorder || unhappiness >= happiness`, so both react one step
*before* the city actually riots:

* `AICityManager` (`secureContentment`, `manageSpecialists`) — for specialists;
* `AIBuildingStrategy.isCityUnhappy` — for production priority.

[[The luxury chain and why it is the fragile term]]
== The luxury chain and why it is the fragile term

The chain, in order, *defined in* `cityOutputs`:

. `cityCommerce(city)` — the sum of trade over all worked tiles, plus building
  trade bonuses.
. `effective = commerce × (1 − gov.commercePenalty)` — Communism loses 25 % here.
. `corruption = CityUtils.calculateCorruption(city, civ, effective)` — distance
  from the capital degrades the rest.
. `afterCorruption = max(0, floor(effective − corruption))`.
. `luxury = floor(afterCorruption × luxuryRate / 100)`.

So luxury happiness is bought with *tax revenue*, at a rate the player chooses
per civ, and only from commerce that survives both Communism's penalty and
distance-from-capital corruption. A civ at 100 % tax cannot buy a single point of
luxury happiness at all — `luxuryRate` is 0 by definition in that case.

[[What disorder costs]]
== What disorder costs

*Defined in* `applyCityOutputs` (`EconomicManager.ts:936`) and `TurnManager`.

[cols="1,3"]
|===
| Effect | During disorder

| Tax | *Zero.*
| Science | *Zero.*
| Commerce (the reported total) | *Zero.*
| Luxury | **Kept.** Deliberately — see below.
| Food eaten | **Yes, in full.** Citizens eat during riots.
| Growth | *Halted.* `processCityGrowth(city, inDisorder)` only grows when the
  city is not in disorder.
| Starvation | **Still happens.** Disorder does not protect a city from losing a
  citizen to an empty food box.
| Shields / production | *Continues.* Explicitly not halted — see below.
| Tile yields | Unchanged; the tiles are still worked.
|===

Two of those are deliberate design decisions worth stating, because both were
once the opposite and both caused a death spiral:

* **Luxury survives disorder.** Zeroing it would strand a city that cannot
  generate enough commerce to earn its way out — a low-trade map would have had
  no way back. See the comment in `processTurn`.
* **Production continues during disorder.** Halting it was tried; it deadlocked
  low-commerce economies forever, since the only cure for disorder is wealth.

=== On leaving disorder

When `city.disorder` transitions from true to false, `TurnManager` calls
`economicManager.recomputeCityYields(city)` — the city is re-assigned its tiles
immediately, so production and food resume on the same turn rather than waiting
for the next growth event.

=== Player feedback

* A 🔥 is drawn under any city in disorder (`MapRenderer.ts:2405`).
* A `CITY_DISORDER` event fires **on transitions only** (never every turn), and
  `EngineEventHandlers` raises the "Sire! The citizens of X are rioting!" modal
  with the mirrored "ORDER RESTORED" message (`GameModals.tsx:2033`).
* The rates modal labels the luxury slider *"Commerce → happiness (prevents
  disorder)"* (`RatesModal.tsx:156`) — the only player-facing hint that luxury
  is a happiness lever.
* The city screen shows the stored `city.happiness` / `city.unhappiness` and a
  disorder flag.

[[AI: garrison duty]]
== AI: garrison duty

Every city keeps a minimum garrison, because a garrison is simultaneously the
cheapest content citizen and the thing that makes the city defensible.

`AIManager.findGarrisonPost(unit, armyGroups)` is consulted for a combat unit
*before* it is offered to an army group, so idle capacity goes to the walls
before it goes to the campaign.

[cols="1,1"]
|===
| Situation | Minimum garrison per city

| Peacetime, or no offensive under way | *1*
| An army group is `marching` or `attacking` | *2*
|===

* The minimum counts *fortified* units beside the city as well as units on the
  tile, because that is the same count the happiness rule uses. Filling a deficit
  the city cannot feel would be pointless.
* Only units **not** already committed to a march are pulled in. A soldier in a
  `marching` group is one the campaign has; taking it back would undo the army
  planner. Raising the floor to 2 during an offensive is what stops a civ
  stripping its cities bare the moment it wins a war.
* The emptiest walls are served first.
* A unit already holding a city that is still below its floor is left where it
  is, rather than sent to a different city — otherwise two garrisons shuffle
  between neighbouring cities every turn.
* Barbarians are excluded. They run on `BarbarianManager`, have no government and
  no tax rate, so martial law buys them nothing; a city in barbarian hands is
  looted, not garrisoned.

Before this existed, garrisons were an accident: a unit with nothing else to do
would fortify beside a city, and an empire that spent every soldier on armies
could end the game with undefended cities and no martial law anywhere.

[[AI: keeping cities calm]]
== AI: keeping cities calm

The AI has three separate systems that care about happiness. They are listed
here because they are the practical answer to "why is my civ rioting".

=== 1. The governor (`AICityManager`)

`manageCity` runs, in this order:

. `secureFood` — *first*, because `secureContentment` can staff an Entertainer
  and an Entertainer is a worker removed from the fields.
. `secureContentment` — hires an Entertainer when the city is unhappy, under
  two different rules:

[cols="1,2"]
|===
| City state | Rule

| *In disorder*
| Always hire one, even at the cost of food. Disorder is a total loss, so it
  always beats a food deficit.

| Unhappy but not disordering
| Only with food headroom: surplus ≥ `AI_MIN_FOOD_SURPLUS (1)` +
  `ENTERTAINER_FOOD_HEADROOM (2)`, i.e. 3.
|===

  Cap: `MAX_ENTERTAINERS_PER_CITY = 2`.

  Unneeded Entertainers are sent back to the fields.
. `steerSurplus` / `manageSpecialists` — and `manageSpecialists` refuses to hire
  a Taxman or Scientist while the city is unhappy or short of food, so happiness
  work never competes with keeping people fed.

The ordering and the two-tier entertainer rule both exist because of a measured
failure: gating the entertainer on food alone blocked the one specialist that
could keep a *disordered* city's output alive.

=== 2. The luxury rate (`AIEconomicManager.luxuryNeedPct`)

The AI computes the luxury percentage its worst city needs:

* for every city: `nonLuxHappiness = specLuxury + martialLawBonus +
  entertainerPotential + 2`, where `entertainerPotential` assumes up to 2 more
  Entertainers at 2 each, and the `+2` is base contentment;
* `need = max(0, population − nonLuxHappiness)` per city, taking the **worst**
  city;
* the percentage is `ceil(need × 100 / minAfterCommerce)` over the cities,
  where `minAfterCommerce` is the smallest post-corruption commerce any city has
  — so the AI never sets a luxury rate its poorest city cannot cash in.

It is then clamped: `min(luxuryNeed, 30, 100 − AI_MIN_TAX − AI_SCIENCE_FLOOR)`,
and dropped to 0 entirely when no city has a happiness problem.

=== 3. Production (`AutoProduction`)

* `isHappinessCrisis(city)` — true when the civ's luxury rate is **≥ 40 %** or
  the city is in disorder. A city that is spending 40 %+ of its commerce
  papering over unhappiness, or is actually rioting, builds a happiness building
  *ahead of everything else*, including defenders.
* A "disorder emergency" also fires when the city's own trade is 0 while it is
  unhappy — the cure is a happiness building, not another ship.
* If no happiness building is unlocked yet, the fallback is a **settler**: a
  settler that completes consumes one citizen, and the citizen that leaves takes
  its unhappiness with it. That is the Civ 1 pressure valve for a crowded,
  dissatisfied city.
* `AIBuildingStrategy.isHappinessEmergency` ranks happiness buildings for a city
  in disorder above all other production.

== AI: what a happiness building is worth

The building coordinator prices happiness by *measuring* the city rather than
reading the table (`BuildingCoordinator.ts`, `evaluateBuildingForCity`): it
recomputes the city's output with and without the building.

[cols="1,3"]
|===
| Situation | How relief is valued

| Relief completes the set (city leaves disorder)
| Worth the city's *whole* output: `max(gold + beakers, MIN_DISORDER_CITY_WORTH)`.

| Partial relief (city is still short)
| Worth the *pro-rata share* of that output:
  `relief / shortfallBefore × cityWorth`. The engine's disorder is all-or-nothing,
  so one Temple does not literally return a fifth of the city's income — but it
  is one step of a purchase the city is going to make anyway.
|===

[IMPORTANT]
====
The pro-rata rule exists because of a measured regression. Valuing a Temple only
by whether it rescued the city *on its own* left cities five points short buying
none at all, and therefore in disorder for the rest of the game — losing all tax,
all science and all growth. Partial relief is priced so the city can climb out.
====

A building that produces no measurable gain is *demoted* to a fifth of its
priority rather than banned, so a city with nothing better to do can still build
it. `city_walls`, `barracks`, `sdi_defense` and `harbor` are exempt from that
demotion entirely: their value is military, which the economic measurement
cannot see. (Exempting them is not optional — when walls were demoted, barbarian
raids wiped every AI civ off the map by turn 135.)

[[AI: government target]]
== AI: government target

*Defined in* `GovernmentManager`.

The AI rules under a fixed ladder and nothing else:

[source]
----
despotism → monarchy → communism
----

* **Republic is never adopted.** It is absent from `AI_GOVERNMENT_LADDER`, the
  candidate set that both `bestGovernmentForCiv` and `evaluateGovernmentForCiv`
  consider. It is unlocked
  and it scores *well* — with tolerance gone it grants +2 happiness and no
  commerce penalty against Communism's +1 and −25 % — so it has to be excluded by
  policy rather than left to the arithmetic. A civ that stops there never reaches
  Communism.
* **Communism is the destination, not a comparison.** Once the tech is researched
  the AI moves, whatever the −25 % commerce penalty says. Left to the score, a
  small or poor civ sat in Monarchy for the rest of the game.
* Anarchy is the punishment for switching (`ANARCHY_TURNS = 3` turns of zeroed
  rates), never a destination.

Because a government's happiness bonus is now the *only* thing it does for a
crowded city, `scoreGovernmentForCiv` weights that bonus by city size and
population — the weight tolerance used to share.

== Caps: how many happiness buildings a city may hold

*Defined in* `BuildingCoordinator.HAPPINESS_BUILDINGS_PER_CITY` = **4**,
enforced across the whole happiness-building set (Temple, Colosseum, Cathedral,
Hospital) in any mix, plus a per-type cap of 3 Temples.

Raising the set cap to 6 was tried and reverted: it lets a civ buy its way out
of disorder with buildings at 1–2 gold upkeep each, and with a dozen cities that
bankrupts the treasury — a worse economy than the disorder it cured. The cheap
sources (Entertainers, luxury, martial law) are what carry a crowded city now, and
the cap exists to stop buildings joining in.

== Two implementations, one live

There is a second, much simpler happiness model in `src/game/City.ts`
(`calculateHappiness`): building happiness only — no base contentment, no luxury,
no specialists, no martial law, no government bonus.

It is **legacy**. Nothing imports that module, and the only place that
instantiates the class (`City.deserialize`) has no callers; the live engine uses
plain `City` objects from `types/game.ts` and `EconomicManager` exclusively. It
is a trap for anyone reading the codebase, because its numbers disagree with the
game: a size-1 city is *content* in the real model and *disordered* in that one.

[[Known gaps]]
== Known gaps

Places where this document describes behaviour that is implemented but *not*
pinned by a test, so a future change could silently alter it:

* **Disorder halts growth but not production, and does not prevent starvation.**
  Implemented in `TurnManager.processCityGrowth(city, inDisorder)`. Both halves
  were deliberate reversions of earlier behaviour (halting production deadlocked
  low-commerce economies; protecting a city from starvation trapped it in a
  spiral), and neither is directly tested.
* **`martialLawMax` is keyed on the government *name***, lowercased, rather than
  on a field in `GovernmentProperties`. Renaming a government in the data would
  silently change martial law with no type error.
* **City Walls' `-1` happiness** has no dedicated test; it is only implicit in
  the worked examples above.
* The preview path (`previewEconomy`, used by the rates modal) re-implements the
  disorder check in a third place. It is correct today and mirrors `cityHappiness`,
  but it is a copy, not a call.
* **The AI does not yet take the martial-law deal.** It garrisons for *defence*
  within two tiles of a city, which earns no martial law at all, and then hires
  Entertainers — a worker off the fields — to do a job a soldier would do for
  free. An attempt to fix this by steering idle combat units onto city tiles was
  measured and reverted: see the note below.

== Open: the AI does not take the martial-law deal, and why that is unproven

Martial law is free happiness that the AI leaves on the table. It garrisons for
*defence* within two tiles of a city — which earns no martial law, because the
test is an exact tile match — and then hires Entertainers, at the cost of a
worker off the fields, to do a job a soldier would do for free.

The obvious fix is to steer idle combat units onto city tiles that still have
room under their cap and are short of happiness. That was implemented (a
`findMartialLawGarrisonTarget` hook in `AIManager.chooseAITarget`, plus a
`martialLawCanFinish` check in `AICityManager.secureContentent` that stops
paying for an Entertainer when a soldier could finish the job) and then
**reverted**, because a single-seed late-game measurement got worse.

[WARNING]
====
That reasoning is **not** established, and this entry records the uncertainty
rather than a conclusion:

* The hook sits ahead of the army-group and retreat logic, so on that seed every
  combat unit re-targeted at a city tile whenever any city was unhappy, and the
  measured result was bad (18 cities, zero military units, 16 in disorder).
* A follow-up trace appeared to explain it — 102 units disbanded and 32
  buildings liquidated by turn 120 — but that trace was taken from an
  *instrumented* run which monkey-patched `processTurn`, consuming the seeded
  RNG differently. It is therefore a different trajectory, not evidence about the
  clean run. The clean suite is green (1410/1410) both with and without the
  *engine* changes.
* So the honest position: the late-game AI-vs-AI scenario on a pinned map has
  enough run-to-run variance that one seed cannot attribute a cause. The hook is
  out only because it was not proven, not because it was proven harmful.
====

To settle it, measure across several seeds rather than one: run the pinned
scenario with and without the hook, three seeds each, and compare the disorder
